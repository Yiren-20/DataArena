EgoScope: A Unified Framework for Characterizing Egocentric Data for World Action Models

Evaluating data quality, distribution, and downstream learning utility.

Work in Progress · Results updated November 19, 2026

Abstract

Abstract

EgoScope evaluates egocentric human data through data health assessment, distribution analysis and controlled World Action Model transfer. Across five datasets and 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, transferring both human-pretrained experts reduces success from 59.7% to 28.6–35.7%. Keeping the original video expert while transferring the action expert yields 59.8–63.2%, with no significant gain over backbone in the current task-paired comparisons. Distribution profiles reveal pronounced differences in diversity and redundancy, while zero-shot video prediction and robot control give different assessments of transfer utility.

Method

Methodology

EgoScope compares human egocentric datasets through three modules. Modules 1 and 2 produce complementary dataset scores: recording and annotation quality, and distributional structure. Module 3 measures downstream learning utility through controlled WAM pretraining and robot post-training. We report these dimensions separately rather than combining them into a single overall score.

MODULE 01 · QUALITY SCORING

Video, caption and hand-pose reliability

Screen existing hand-pose annotations for jumps, discontinuities and physically inconsistent motion. Verify candidate anomalies using exactly five consecutive video frames centered on the suspect frame. Refine the verifier with a small budget of human feedback rather than replacing the annotation pipeline.

Scoring outputs: per-check video failure rates, caption accuracy and missing-caption rate, and verified pose-annotation error rates, with applicability and review evidence retained. Verifier performance is assessed against human-verified held-out labels using precision, recall and F1. The automated checks below are preliminary. The structural-format check needs validation, and VLM scoring is not included in these results.
MODULE 02 · DISTRIBUTION SCORING

Diversity, coverage and domain gap

Score visual and language diversity, cross-episode redundancy and cross-dataset distribution gaps using a common representation and matched sampling. Intrinsic metrics include Vendi scores, effective rank and nearest-neighbor redundancy; shared-space comparisons use cluster occupancy, Jensen–Shannon divergence and MMD². These form a distribution profile rather than a single ranking. Target-conditioned coverage is a separate planned extension.

Measured sample: five 150-hour dataset subsets, with 20,000 two-second windows and 8 frames per window for each dataset. Encoder: SigLIP2-base-p16-224. Visual and caption profiles describe the sampled data under this representation.
MODULE 03 · DOWNSTREAM LEARNING UTILITY

Controlled FastWAM transfer validation

Pretrain both the Wan2.2-TI2V-5B video expert and ActionDiT action expert on each of five human datasets for 60,000 steps, with approximately 145 hours of video exposure per dataset. Compare a backbone initialization with both-expert and action-only transfer.

Robot post-training. 50 tasks × 50 clean-scene demonstrations, 6,000 continuous steps, effective batch 128, learning rate 10−4 with 5% warmup and cosine decay. All parameters train in every condition. Human-pretrained initializations reset action input/output layers and the proprioception encoder to accommodate the change from human hands to ALOHA joints.

Experiments and Results

Five egocentric human datasets, eleven model initializations and 27,500 clean-scene evaluation rollouts. Main results use continuous 6,000-step post-training with one training seed per condition. Source update: November 19, 2026.

Training schedule correction

The earlier 3,000 + 3,000-step runs reset AdamW state and restarted learning-rate warmup at the midpoint. All eleven conditions were rerun with 6,000 uninterrupted steps. The restart underestimated action-only transfer by approximately 3.8 pp on average. The large both-expert degradation persists under both schedules. The continuous runs above supersede the earlier two-dataset pilot on this page.

Findings

Both-expert transfer consistently degrades robot control

Across five human datasets, success falls by 24.0–31.2 pp relative to backbone. Restoring the original video expert brings performance back near baseline. This initialization ablation implicates the human-pretrained video expert and its interaction with robot post-training. The underlying mechanism remains open.

Action-only transfer preserves performance, with uncertain gains

All five action-only point estimates exceed backbone by 0.1–3.5 pp, but every baseline-comparison interval includes zero. Gen-HumanEgo has the highest observed success at 63.2%. Additional training seeds are needed to assess reproducibility.

Diversity and redundancy expose differences that do not fully explain transfer

EgoDex has lower visual diversity and higher repetition, alongside weaker both-expert transfer. However, visually similar OpenAoE and EgoStandard differ by 7.0 pp in that condition. Motion, action semantics and adaptation dynamics require further investigation.

Better zero-shot video prediction can coexist with worse control

MicroAGI and OpenAoE reduce zero-shot video loss by about 37%, yet their both-expert initializations lose approximately 24 pp of robot success. The observed transfer problem cannot be explained solely by poor zero-shot prediction of robot images.

Dataset comparisons and statistical scope

For both-expert transfer, MicroAGI, OpenAoE and Gen-HumanEgo are within 0.5 pp of each other. Each exceeds EgoDex and EgoStandard in the supplied pairwise task-bootstrap comparisons. For action-only transfer, one of ten unadjusted pairwise intervals excludes zero: Gen-HumanEgo versus MicroAGI (+3.4 pp, [0.5, 6.2]). Multiple comparisons and a single training seed limit interpretation.

Study scope and next measurements

Five datasets, one FastWAM architecture, one training seed per condition and clean-scene evaluation only. The task-bootstrap intervals exclude training-seed variation. Randomized-scene evaluations were reported as started on November 19, and their outcomes are not included here. The distribution analysis does not yet include RoboTwin itself. Causal effects of data diversity, motion and annotation quality require controlled interventions.

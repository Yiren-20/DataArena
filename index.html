Abstract

Abstract

How do human egocentric datasets differ, and how do those differences manifest in World Action Model (WAM) learning and robot transfer? EgoScope examines this question through a comparative study of EgoDex and MicroAGI, using three modules: data health scoring, distribution scoring and controlled downstream transfer evaluation. Matched distribution analysis reveals higher visual and language diversity, lower cross-episode redundancy and largely distinct cluster occupancy in MicroAGI relative to EgoDex. Under the same FastWAM pretraining and robot post-training recipe, the datasets also produce different transfer outcomes. Action-expert transfer achieves 70.6% robot task success with MicroAGI and 66.9% with EgoDex, compared with a 65.6% baseline. Both-expert transfer reverses this ordering, yielding 36.2% for MicroAGI and 50.0% for EgoDex. MicroAGI additionally achieves lower zero-shot video denoising loss, but this advantage does not persist as better downstream control under both-expert transfer. These comparisons characterize dataset differences across representation and learning outcomes, showing that their relative utility depends on the transferred model components. Diversity is one observed difference among several; the present study does not isolate its causal contribution.

Method

Methodology

EgoScope compares human egocentric datasets through three modules. Modules 1 and 2 produce complementary dataset scores: recording and annotation quality, and distributional structure. Module 3 measures downstream learning utility through controlled WAM pretraining and robot post-training. We report these dimensions separately rather than combining them into a single overall score.

MODULE 01 · QUALITY SCORING

Video, caption and hand-pose reliability

Screen existing hand-pose annotations for jumps, discontinuities and physically inconsistent motion. Verify candidate anomalies using exactly five consecutive video frames centered on the suspect frame. Refine the verifier with a small budget of human feedback rather than replacing the annotation pipeline.

Scoring outputs: per-check video failure rates, caption accuracy and missing-caption rate, and verified pose-annotation error rates, with applicability and review evidence retained. Verifier performance is assessed against human-verified held-out labels using precision, recall and F1. Quantitative quality results are not reported on this page yet.
MODULE 02 · DISTRIBUTION SCORING

Diversity, coverage and domain gap

Score visual and language diversity, cross-episode redundancy and cross-dataset distribution gaps using a common representation and matched sampling. Intrinsic metrics include Vendi scores, effective rank and nearest-neighbor redundancy; shared-space comparisons use cluster occupancy, Jensen–Shannon divergence and MMD². These form a distribution profile rather than a single ranking. Target-conditioned coverage is a separate planned extension.

Measured sample: 20,000 windows per dataset, drawn from 18,395 EgoDex and 17,688 MicroAGI episodes. Each window spans 2 seconds with 8 frames. Encoder: SigLIP2 base, patch 16, 224-pixel input; 768-dimensional mean-pooled visual features. Results below describe this sample and representation.
MODULE 03 · DOWNSTREAM LEARNING UTILITY

Controlled FastWAM transfer validation

Pretrain both the Wan2.2 5B video expert and ActionDiT 1.02B action expert on EgoDex or MicroAGI, with equal training steps and samples. Post-train using 800 RoboTwin 2.0 demonstrations, then compare initializations that transfer the human-pretrained action expert alone or both experts.

Protocol: 60,000 pretraining steps, effective batch 64; 2,000 post-training steps, effective batch 128; 16 robot tasks, 10 rollouts each. In the action-only transfer condition, the video expert is initialized from the original model but is still trained during post-training.

Experiments and Results

Our experiments compare EgoDex and MicroAGI at two levels: their sampled content distributions and their effects under a common downstream training protocol. We report distribution metrics, robot task success and video/action losses to identify where the datasets differ and whether their relative ordering persists across measurements.

2. Downstream robot task success

Training protocol and data preprocessing

Both datasets use center-crop resizing to 224 × 224 and the same 33-frame training window, containing 32 action steps and 9 temporally sampled video frames. Full-model pretraining updates the 5.00B Wan2.2 video expert and 1.02B ActionDiT expert for 60,000 steps with effective batch size 64: 3.84 million sampled windows per dataset. Compute and sample counts are matched rather than dataset hours or epochs. The corresponding training-pool coverage is 160.8 hours for EgoDex and 149.3 hours for MicroAGI.

Robot post-training uses 16 tasks × 50 clean demonstrations (800 total), 2,000 steps, effective batch size 128 and learning rate 10⁻⁴ with cosine scheduling. All experts remain trainable. Action input/output layers and the proprioception encoder are reinitialized for human-pretrained checkpoints. Evaluation uses 10 clean-scene rollouts per task, with shared evaluation seeds and instructions across conditions. “Action-expert transfer” describes initialization, not freezing.

With equal pretraining sample counts and training steps, MicroAGI action-expert transfer reaches 70.6% success, compared with 65.6% without human pretraining and 66.9% with EgoDex. Transferring both experts yields 36.2% for MicroAGI and 50.0% for EgoDex. All conditions use the same 800 robot demonstrations and post-training recipe.

Statistical comparisons and evaluation scope

Task-paired comparisons use 16 tasks, 10 rollouts per task and one training seed per condition. MicroAGI action-expert transfer improves the observed mean by 5.0 pp versus baseline (paired t = 1.20; 95% CI −3.9 to +13.9 pp), and by 3.8 pp versus EgoDex (paired t = 0.92; 95% CI −4.9 to +12.4 pp). These positive differences are not statistically significant in the current evaluation. Both-expert transfer declines are significant in the reported task-paired tests (EgoDex: t = −5.2; MicroAGI: t = −7.9). Diversity is measured independently; this two-dataset comparison does not isolate its causal effect.

Findings: how the datasets differ

1. The datasets occupy distinct visual and language distributions.

Under matched sampling, MicroAGI has higher visual Vendi (113.12 versus 88.57), more distinct captions (16,876 versus 2,215) and lower cross-episode near-duplicate rates (1.90% versus 16.43% at τ = 0.95). Only 5 of 100 shared-space clusters contain windows from both datasets. The comparison therefore involves substantial differences in content and redundancy, rather than two interchangeable samples of human activity.

2. Their downstream ordering depends on the transferred expert.

MicroAGI yields higher observed success than EgoDex under action-expert transfer (70.6% versus 66.9%) and improves on the 65.6% baseline by 5.0 percentage points. Under both-expert transfer, MicroAGI instead yields lower success (36.2% versus 50.0%). Neither dataset has a uniformly superior transfer outcome across the evaluated initializations.

3. Dataset differences in video loss do not predict their control ranking.

MicroAGI has lower zero-shot video loss than EgoDex (0.312 versus 0.677), yet lower robot success when both pretrained experts are transferred. After 2,000 post-training steps, both have higher logged video loss than baseline, while final action losses differ only modestly despite large differences in rollout success. Dataset comparisons therefore require downstream evaluation alongside prediction losses.

Interpretation and open questions

The distribution analysis and transfer experiments describe complementary aspects of the EgoDex–MicroAGI difference. Higher diversity accompanies higher observed action-transfer success, but the datasets also differ in scenes, task composition, episode duration and recording conditions. The present comparison does not identify which factor causes the transfer gap. Controlled data interventions and additional datasets are needed to separate these factors. Format adaptation, viewpoint shift and changes in action-relevant representations remain hypotheses for the video-expert transfer behavior.

Scope: two human datasets; one training seed per condition; 16 clean-scene tasks with 10 evaluation rollouts each; 800 demonstrations and 2,000 robot post-training steps. The action-transfer difference between datasets is not statistically significant in the current task-paired test; their both-expert difference is significant.
